Navitas Semiconductor Corp. is the favorite among investors betting on the next phase of the artificial-intelligence boom. It generates less than $50 million in annual revenue and is valued at more than $4 billion — almost 107 times forward sales, compared with about 22 times sales for Nvidia Corp. and about 13 times for Advanced Micro Devices Inc., according to data compiled by Bloomberg.

The Torrance, California-based chipmaker makes power chips that help control how electricity moves through servers. Its main products use gallium nitride, a material designed to convert electricity with less energy loss and heat than traditional silicon. That makes the company relevant to next-generation data centers, where operators are moving toward higher-voltage designs, including 800-volt systems, to deliver more power through crowded server racks.

That positioning attracts investors who believe the shift to higher-voltage data centers will make Navitas's chips essential. Navitas shares surged about 144% this year, compared with a roughly 13% gain for the Russell 2000 Index and a 61% advance for the Russell 2000 semiconductor group, as analysts expect the company to remain unprofitable through at least 2027.

Within the Russell 2000 semiconductor index, companies tied to how electricity is delivered and managed inside data centers have led gains. Sunnyvale, California-based Alpha & Omega Semiconductor Ltd., a maker of power-control chips, climbed about 102% this year, while Plano, Texas-based Diodes Inc. is up roughly 105%.

At 107.4x forward sales, Navitas is priced not as a component maker, but as a grid-scale AI bottleneck.

"Power is our single biggest constraint," Amazon.com Inc. Chief Executive Officer Andy Jassy said on a second-quarter earnings call last year.

In other words, companies have the money and the plans, and not enough electricity to plug everything in.

Data centers consumed about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity globally in 2024, or roughly 1.5% of total demand, according to the International Energy Agency. That figure is projected to reach about 945 terawatt-hours by 2030, more than doubling from current levels and approaching the scale of Japan’s total electricity consumption.

A single cabinet of servers, about the size of a household refrigerator, may use as much as 1 megawatt of power by 2028, up from about 40 kilowatts in 2023, according to UBS Group AG. Each cabinet would need about 25 times more electricity than it does today.

To handle that increase, data centers are redesigning how electricity is delivered to those machines. Instead of sending power in small amounts and converting it, newer systems move larger amounts of electricity directly, reducing energy loss and the amount of wiring needed. These designs often use higher-voltage power, in some cases around 800 volts, compared with about 48 volts in traditional systems. UBS estimates this can cut wiring by about 45% while delivering significantly more power through the same infrastructure.

For a large data center, the shift can reduce equipment and power-related costs by hundreds of millions of dollars and as much as $1 billion over time. At the same time, the cost of the components that handle electricity inside each cabinet could rise from about $4,000 today to more than $100,000 in next-generation systems. That is shifting more of the system’s value toward companies that control how electricity is delivered, rather than those that design the computing chips.

Power constraints are changing when decisions are made in the buildout of AI infrastructure. Developers can wait years to secure electricity, and key equipment needed to deliver power is also slow to arrive. Large transformers, for example, can take four to five years to deliver, according to UBS.

That forces companies to act earlier. On a recent earnings call, Oracle Corp. said it had secured up to 2.8 gigawatts of power supply for future data centers, equivalent to the electricity needs of more than 2 million homes, capacity it does not yet need, for buildings it has not yet built.

The same logic extends to chips. Once a company commits to a power architecture, it must also decide which semiconductors will run inside that system, because the two choices are inseparable. That means chip suppliers are being selected at the design stage, sometimes two or three years before a rack ships. In previous cycles, demand appeared first in orders, then in revenue, and finally in stock prices. In the current cycle, the sequence has compressed: by the time construction begins, the supplier list is already set.

“The market is starting to price where companies sit in the value chain, rather than what they earn today,” UBS analysts wrote in a recent note.

The dynamic helps explain why companies tied to power delivery are attracting attention before revenue has materialized. Nvidia, which is redesigning the electrical plumbing inside its next-generation data-center racks to run on 800 volts of direct current — about six times the voltage of a standard American wall outlet — has named Navitas as one of three chip suppliers for the new system, alongside Texas Instruments and STMicroelectronics.

At the same time, demand expectations may be running ahead of reality. UBS estimates that industry plans imply more than 40 gigawatts of new data-center capacity over the next two years, compared with the 10 to 15 gigawatts it considers more realistic.

Suppliers are wary of “double ordering,” which means customers placing overlapping orders with multiple suppliers to secure scarce components. Monolithic Power Systems, one of the main suppliers of voltage regulator chips to AI server racks, told investors it was struggling to determine “what's real demand and what may be some double ordering” from customers trying to lock in supply. Munters Group, a maker of data-center cooling equipment, reported orders seven times higher than shipments in a single quarter, but said it could not tell how much of that reflected genuine need.

That leaves a gap between expectations and execution and Navitas sits squarely inside it. The company generated about $46 million in revenue over the past year and has been losing more than $12 million per quarter, having abandoned its older business selling chips into smartphones in order to focus entirely on data centers. It expects volume production for 800-volt systems to begin in 2027, the same year Bank of America analysts say large-scale deployment is unlikely to arrive before. The bet investors are making is not that Navitas has already won. It is that the window will open before the money runs out.
