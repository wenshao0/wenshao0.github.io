Biotech Losing Over 99%, Squeezed by Slower FDA Reviews

Bluebird Bio Inc. was a favorite among biotechnology investors in December 2017 because of its commitment to cure sickle-cell disease -- traditionally treated with frequent blood transfusions before most patients die in their 40s -- with a single injection. Most of the 16 analysts researching the Somerville, Massachusetts company recommended its stock, then approaching a record valuation of almost $10 billion. The same shares are worth less than $30 million today with no cure in sight and just one analyst among six who follow the maker of gene therapies for genetic disorders insisting they're a buying opportunity.

Biotech, especially firms with a median market capitalization of $776 million, is the biggest casualty of the small and midcap companies that make up the Russell 2000. Nine of the 10 worst-performers in the index during the past decade, including Bluebird, are biotech and each has lost more than 99% of its market value, according to data compiled by Bloomberg. The collapsing valuations coincide with fewer approvals by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration which has the commanding role determining whether a future product can advance through clinical development to the public market.

For some firms, the turning point came when a lead candidate failed to gain FDA approval. For others, problems emerged during clinical trials because of safety issues, lack of efficacy or regulatory holds. Seattle-based Atossa Therapeutics, for example, received an FDA warning letter in 2013 concerning its breast health testing device. The company was forced to recall the product, and its share price fell nearly 80% from March to October that year.

Small biotech companies rely on hitting key regulatory milestones to secure continued financing. As the FDA slows reviews and cuts staff under a broader government restructuring, the pressures facing these fragile models are intensifying.

A Business Model that Can't Wait

These early-stage biotech firms typically lack diversified pipelines or recurring revenue streams. Regulatory milestones, such as completing a clinical trial or advancing to an FDA meeting, are critical for securing the next round of funding.

Argenx SE, a Netherlands-based biotech company, secured FDA approval in late 2021 for Vyvgart, a treatment for generalized myasthenia gravis, a rare neuromuscular disease. Soon after receiving approval, Argenx completed a $700 million public offering that was significantly oversubscribed. Its stock price, around $350 at the end of 2021, rose to about $621 by April 2025, a gain of about 77 percent.

But few did.

According to FTI Consulting, a Washington, D.C.-based global advisory firm specializing in corporate restructuring and biotech industry finance, by 2024, more than half of the biotech firms that went public during the 2020 to 2021 boom were trading below their offering prices. Among the 111 companies still listed, the average cumulative stock return was minus 65 percent, and more than 40 percent had market values lower than their cash holdings. Nearly all of them continued to operate at a loss in 2024, with negative earnings even before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization.

San Diego-based Regulus Therapeutics Inc. once developed a hepatitis C treatment called RG-101, which used microRNA technology. In 2016, clinical trials were halted after some patients showed signs of liver damage. The FDA placed the program on hold and asked for additional safety data. Regulus was unable to gather the data before running out of cash. By 2017, it discontinued the hepatitis C program and shifted to kidney disease research but never recovered its previous market value.

Michael Rachlin, co-leader of FTI’s Life Sciences Corporate Finance practice, said the probability that a preclinical drug candidate will reach commercialization is less than 10 percent.

A separate analysis led by Michael Hay, a pharmaceutical industry analyst whose earlier work on clinical success rates became a widely used benchmark in biotech financing and regulatory risk assessment, found that the clinical success rate was 10.8 percent. The average development cost per approved therapy was about $6.1 billion, with an average timeline of 14 years.

Small Biotechs Face More FDA Delays and Rejections

The regulatory environment for small biotech companies has become increasingly difficult.

A review of FDA advisory committee activities found that the number of new drug applications reviewed by external committees dropped from 59 percent in 2011 to 6 percent in 2021, according to a study published in Health Affairs. The authors noted that the reduction in advisory committee reviews could affect regulatory transparency and stakeholder engagement, factors that are critical for early-stage biotech firms seeking to navigate the approval process and attract financing.

Another analysis from Evervana, a Chicago, Illinois–based consulting firm specializing in regulatory strategy and biotech risk assessment, showed that small-cap companies, those with market capitalizations below $300 million, faced a 78 percent chance of regulatory delay or rejection. In comparison, companies valued above $300 million faced a 47 percent chance.

Over the past decade, the NASDAQ Biotechnology Index (IBB), which tracks large- and mid-cap biotech companies, has delivered a total return of 12.99 percent, according to Bloomberg data.

FDA Staff Cuts Slow Drug Reviews

In April 2025, the Department of Health and Human Services announced plans to eliminate about 3,500 positions at the FDA as part of a broader federal workforce reduction. Although the department said the cuts would not affect drug, medical device, or food reviewers, nor inspectors, several high-profile departures suggest otherwise. The layoffs included Chief Medical Officer Dr. Hilary Marston and Dr. Peter Stein, who led the agency’s Office of New Drugs. Dr. Peter Marks, who oversaw vaccines and biologics, was also among those let go.

While the full operational impact remains unclear, biotech firms have already reported delays in FDA interactions.

In a letter to Congress, biotech executives said FDA meetings were being postponed, citing multiple examples of regulatory delays. One Massachusetts-based biotech said its dispute resolution process collapsed after FDA senior reviewers became unavailable. A Boston-based biotech managing a clinical hold on one program and seeking guidance on another said it was unsure whether its assigned FDA project managers were still with the agency. The company confirmed that its toxicology reviewer, who had been familiar with the program, had left, resulting in anticipated delays.

Executives also pointed to broader gaps in policy and legal support. Key individuals across the Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) and the Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research (CBER), including legal staff who work closely with scientific reviewers, were among those departing. Industry leaders said that the loss of these senior figures could weaken the FDA’s ability to handle complex, emerging technologies, where interactive dialogue between policy experts and scientific reviewers is critical.

“When you cut the administrative staff and you still have these product deadlines, you are creating an unwinnable situation,” said Marc Scheineson, a former FDA associate commissioner, in an interview with the Wall Street Journal. The worst thing for companies isn’t getting guidance when needed and following all the steps for approval, only to “prepare a $100 million application and get denied because of something that could’ve been communicated or resolved before the trial was under way,” Scheineson said.

When the FDA Clock Slows, Small Biotech Struggles

For small biotechs with limited cash runways and a dependence on steady regulatory progress, even modest delays can jeopardize survival.

Executives said that postponed meetings, inconsistent feedback and longer review cycles are delaying clinical development schedules and threatening future funding rounds.

“The extreme turmoil in the biotech financial markets reflects that investors are worried about our ability to execute,” executives wrote in the letter to Congress. “Any delays in FDA reviews will substantially impact these companies’ ability to secure the funding they need to continue to advance to the next stage of development.” Signatories included X4 Pharmaceuticals Chief Executive Officer Paula Ragan and Regulus Therapeutics Vice President Brian Campion.
